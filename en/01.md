# Chapter 1

Two women were sitting by the window and talking so loudly that Natalie knew what a room with a sea view cost. The one facing into the restaurant named the figure and laughed, as though she had said something indecent.

Natalie's table was in the corner, right by the kitchen, and every time the waiter shouldered the door open a warm breath came out after him, fried onions and a wet rag. The bulb above her had burned out, and nobody seemed in a hurry to change it; this end of the restaurant, apparently, was not counted at all. She had chosen the place herself, from the doorway, and had not so much as unbuttoned her coat, which was gray, like the sweater underneath. By evening her hair had matted into a knot at the back of her head, and she kept feeling for it without being able to find where it began. A chance glance would have made out nothing in that gloom except a pair of hands above the table, but nobody looked this way.

The pizza was getting cold. She tore off pieces and chewed slowly, with an open malice she saw no reason to hide, and the cheese stretched from her lips to the plate and had to be bitten through. The glass of red, her first in God knows how long, was sour. She drank half of it at once and nursed the rest.

The women were talking about men. One was stingy but fun in bed — they said it just like that, "in bed," and one of them covered her mouth with her hand while the other didn't bother. Another kept bringing flowers, every single week, which was annoying, because you had to find somewhere to put them and change the water, and before you knew it they started to stink. From there they moved on to vacations, to where a person might go to be warm and lie flat out all day long. Somebody had been given a bracelet for an anniversary, and it was the wrong bracelet.

The wrong bracelet, if you please. Natalie picked an olive off the pizza with her fingernail, put it in her mouth, and crushed it against her palate with her tongue.

She looked them over without the least embarrassment. It is easy from the dark, the way at night you watch other people's windows from an unlit room. The one with her back to the glass had lips so full of filler that the upper one hung over the lower like the peak of a cap, and the lipstick on them was a loud, vulgar scarlet, the color of a fire engine. Her forehead was smooth and motionless and plastic, her cheeks shone, and her eyebrows had been drawn on thick and even, the way children draw with a marker. She laughed with her mouth alone; the rest of her face took no part in it.

The other sat half turned toward Natalie. Her features were softer, her voice lower, and she kept touching the bag on the chair beside her, pale smooth leather with a gold fastening that managed to gleam even here. Whenever she laughed, her hand went to the clasp of its own accord and pinned the bag to the seat, as if it might walk off. Natalie noticed this on the third or fourth burst of laughter, and after that she waited for it: a laugh, a grab, a laugh, a grab. She wanted the precious thing to fall at last, to spring open and spill its insides across the dirty floor, with that woman down on her knees under the table, fishing lipstick and coins out from beneath the chairs.

They were her age or a little older. So people still had all that at nearly forty: the sea, the bracelets, the men in bed.

Idiots, both of them, no doubt. It was pleasant to think so, and more pleasant still to imagine that the Pout's husband was sleeping with someone younger, that she knew it, and that this was why she laughed louder than anyone. The other, Natalie supposed, had been fat at school and teased for it, and now she had lost the weight and turned spiteful and was taking her revenge on the world with a handbag. That was going too far, and she knew it, but the thought was as warming as the wine and she had no wish to give it up.

The waiter passed her table for the third time. She raised her hand; either he didn't see it or he saw it and didn't think it worth stopping for. He was carrying two desserts to the window, and as he set them down he leaned in and said something that made both women laugh at once, and the owner of the bag promptly took hold of it.

They had been brought the same thing, a disk of chocolate with a scrap of gold leaf on top. Natalie knew the stuff was edible and tasted of nothing, and still she could not look away as the Pout lifted hers with a spoon and laid it on her tongue like a communion wafer.

She lowered her eyes to her plate, where only crusts remained. Mother said sometimes that only beggars finished them and sometimes that throwing bread away was a sin, and since in thirty-odd years Natalie had never worked out which rule was the real one, she kept both to be safe: in company she left them, and in private she picked at them. Nobody could see her in the corner by the kitchen, so she ate one and pushed the rest away.

Meanwhile, at the window, they had moved on to children. The Pout had a daughter who refused to study what she was supposed to and wanted to draw.

"So what did you do?" the other asked.

"What could I do? Let her draw. It's her life."

Natalie stopped chewing. Over by the window the artist's mother licked her spoon and announced that at sixteen she had wanted to be a singer herself, can you imagine, a singer, and burst out laughing, and her friend joined in and, as usual, reached for the clasp.

The bag rocked, and something soft slid off it and dropped under the table without a sound: a glove, pale, the color of milky coffee, to match the leather. Evidently the pair had been tucked under the strap, and one of them had not held on. Its owner noticed nothing; she was laughing. Natalie could see where the lost thing lay, by the leg of the chair, its fingers pointing at the window. She finished her wine.

The check came the moment they asked for it. They argued a long while over who would pay, and the Pout won, holding her phone to the card reader without a glance at the total. Then followed the business of getting dressed, coats and scarves and car keys that took some finding in the depths of the bag, and her friend, not looking, stuffed into her pocket the single glove left on the chair. Natalie picked up a crust and began crumbling it onto the tablecloth.

When they went out, the bell over the door rang, cold air from the street ran along the floor, and the room grew so quiet that she could hear water running in the kitchen.

She waited a little longer, then got up as though she were going to the restroom, walked past the window, and, pretending to fix her boot, picked up the glove. The leather was thin as tissue paper and still warm on top from the radiator, with a slippery silk lining inside. It smelled of hand cream and of something sweeter, the scent of expensive shops, the ones Natalie never went into. She slipped her find into her coat pocket.

In the restroom she turned on the tap, held her fingers under the water, and raised her eyes to the mirror, above which a light was humming. A woman in gray looked back at her, with a gray face and sauce on her chin. She licked a finger and rubbed the orange spot away. Of course, she could hand the glove to the waiter, who would toss it into the box of lost things along with other people's umbrellas and a child's mitten without its mate.

Back at the table, the pocket seemed to drag, though what was in it weighed nothing at all. The phone said six forty-one; she had come in at ten past six. She looked at the crusts, at the glass with its red ring at the bottom, at the black window that mirrored the room, and saw that her corner was not in it, only a piece of wall and the kitchen door. It was time to go.

She had to go up to the counter for the check. The waiter regarded her as though she had just walked in off the street.

"Which table were you?"

"In the corner."

He looked around for the corner and didn't find it right away. The card reader thought for so long that she had time to picture her account coming up empty and the fact being announced to the whole deserted room. At last the machine beeped: approved. The waiter tore off the receipt and set it in front of her without looking, then went off to the window to put the chairs up on the tables.

***

Outside it was drizzling. It was the end of October, that time of year when night has fallen by seven and the streetlights seem to burn for their own sake. Wet leaves stuck to the pavement slid apart under her soles.

She did not put on her own gray knitted gloves, pilled with wear; they lay in her pocket with the keys, the list, and the other one. Stopping where it was brighter, she unfolded the slip of paper, though she knew it by heart. Mother wrote these notes on the backs of old bills, in large letters, pressing so hard that at every period the pen went clean through.

*Milk (NOT skim)*

*Bread*

*Eggs. Check the dates!!*

*Butter*

*Green apples. GREEN*

*Dish soap*

*Batteries for the remote (not the ones from last time)*

And below, under a line: *Come straight home.*

The supermarket was across the street. Its glass doors slid apart and breathed warm air and music at her, and after the gloom of the restaurant the light hit her eyes so hard that she squinted at the price tags like an old woman.

Taking a basket, she worked her way down the list. The milk took some doing: it couldn't be skim, it had to have the latest date, and the freshest are always pushed to the back, so you have to reach into the cold for them. Next to her a young couple stood over the cheeses; he held her from behind with a finger hooked through the belt loop of her jeans, and she laughed and said, "No, that one stinks." In a year, Natalie decided, he would be holding someone else like that, and she thought it through slowly, with pleasure, giving it the same attention she had given the milk.

Then bread, then eggs. She opened the carton right there by the shelf and turned each one in its hollow with a finger, because once there had been a cracked one and Mother had brought it up for a week. After that, butter. The apples were all yellowish green; she spent a minute over them, picking out the greenest, and knew all the same what she would be told.

By the shelves of cleaning products a little boy lay on the floor and howled, while a woman stood over him with her phone and didn't look down. Walking around them, Natalie thought that this mother knew nothing about raising children and that a child of hers would never have lain on the floor like that. Then it came to her that she wouldn't get the chance to find out, and she took the cheapest dish soap off the shelf, held it a moment, and put it back for one that cost more: Mother called the cheap kind water.

The batteries hung by the registers. She checked them against the slip of paper and still couldn't remember which ones they had bought last time, so she took some at random, certain they would be wrong. That was the end of the list, and she could have gone to pay. Natalie turned toward the back of the store.

There, next to the frozen fish, a refrigerated case was humming. Inside, on three shelves under clear plastic domes, stood chocolate, sponge, and cream cakes, some heaped with berries under a glossy glaze, every one on a cardboard base with a paper doily. One had *With Love* piped across it in icing.

Natalie lingered in front of the case for a long time while its glass gave back her reflection, a woman holding a basket of milk and eggs. A security guard walked past and eyed her, and at that she opened the door, so he wouldn't think she was just standing there. She chose a small one, not the cheapest: white, covered in buttercream, with a single rose in the middle whose edge had begun to melt. There was no writing on it.

The cashier, who had a ring through her eyebrow, rang up everything on the list and the one thing that wasn't, then looked up. Natalie waited for the girl to say something.

"Do you need a bag?"

"Yes."

She settled the cake on top, on the apples, so it wouldn't get crushed, but on the way out she bumped the turnstile anyway and had to stop and look inside. The rose had survived.

It was a twelve-minute walk home. The handles cut into her palm at once, and she had to shift the bag from hand to hand and warm the free one in her pocket, where each time her fingers met the smooth glove that wasn't hers, flinched away, and soon found it again.

At the crosswalk she stepped into a puddle. It lay in the shadow of a billboard, flat and black and no different from the asphalt, and she put her whole foot in it. Ice-cold water poured in over the top of her boot and spread under her toes. She stood like that until the light began to blink, then walked on, squelching at every step.

Her sock was soaked through, and her tights with it; the foot turned numb with cold and then stopped feeling anything at all. The bag knocked against her knee, and her mind went from the eggs, which had probably broken, and the boots, which would have to dry until morning, to the woman with the handbag, who at this moment, this very minute, was sitting in a warm car, feeling under the seat for the other glove, not finding it, and not caring.

At the door of the building she set the bag down on the wet step and reached for her keys. They were at the very bottom of the pocket, under everything else, and when she tugged at the ring the whole lot came out with them: the list, both knitted gloves, and the pale one. The bunch rang against the stone and dropped through a gap in the boot grate, and the rest landed in the water.

Natalie crouched down. She picked up the stranger's glove first, shook it off, wiped it on her coat, blew on it for no reason, and put it away in her pocket, and only then did she gather up her own, sodden and heavy as floor rags. Getting the keys out of the grate with two fingers took a long time, and she scraped a knuckle on the iron. The list was soaked; the bottom line had run, and *Come straight home* had become a blue smear.

When she straightened up, she rested her forehead for a moment against the door, which was cold and smelled of paint. Somewhere upstairs a television was blaring through the whole stairwell, and Natalie knew whose.

The apartment reeked of medicine and something fried; on the screen someone was crying and a studio audience was clapping.

"Finally," Mother said from her room.

Natalie pulled off her boot, and the sock left a damp, dark print on the floor. Mother came out into the hall in her bathrobe, steadying herself against the wall, which there was no need to do.

"I've been alone all evening," she said. "My blood pressure's two hundred, everything's going black. I thought, that's it, I'm going to die and nobody will even know, and you're off somewhere. Do you understand that? There's no one but you to bring me so much as a glass of water."

"I understand."

"You don't understand anything." Mother stroked her wet sleeve. "Are you frozen? My poor darling. Why do they keep you so late? People have no conscience at all."

"I got held up," Natalie said.

Mother unpacked the bag herself, as she always did. She raised the milk to the lamp and read the label, moving her lips; she opened the egg carton and counted; over the apples she let fall only "Well, I see," and added not another word, which was the worst of it. Without looking, she set the batteries aside. The cake came out last, and she kept it at arm's length by the cardboard base, the way you hold something that might drip on your robe.

"What's this?"

"A cake."

"I can see it's a cake. I'm asking what it's for."

Natalie said nothing. She stood in the kitchen doorway on one leg, the wet foot drawn up because the bare floor was so cold.

"What do you need cake for?" Mother set it on the table and pushed it away from herself with one finger. "Have you seen your ass? Pretty soon you won't fit into a single skirt. You won't fit through the door. Who do you think I'm doing all this for, cooking you oatmeal with water, and you go around the shops collecting little cakes. Money doesn't grow on trees, you know."

"Mother," Natalie said. "It's just a cake."

Mother lifted her eyebrows and repeated it to some point past Natalie's shoulder, the corner where the kitten calendar hung:

"Mother. What a word. Like in prison. Not Mom, not Mommy. 'Mother.' I raised her, I went without sleep, I gave her everything I had. And why, I ask you, does my sweetheart treat me like this?"

The kittens on the calendar made no reply, and neither did Natalie. Mother stood a while longer, sighed, and reached into the cabinet for her pills. She kept them in a plastic box divided into the days of the week, and every evening, sorting them out for the next day, she asked the same thing:

"What's the date today?"

"The twenty-ninth," Natalie said, and waited.

Mother shook two white pills and a yellow one into her palm.

"The twenty-ninth," she repeated, and snapped the little lid shut. "So tomorrow's the thirtieth. Wake me at seven, I have to get my blood drawn. And put that cake away, I don't want to see it. And turn off the light in the hall. This isn't a palace."

She went off to her room, and through the wall the television got louder.

Natalie pulled off the other boot, then her tights and socks together, and stood barefoot on the cold floor. She didn't feel like changing or taking a shower, but she did turn off the light in the hall.

The tea in the pot had been made that morning; it had steeped until it was black and had long gone cold. She filled a mug to the brim and took a sip: bitter as bark. From behind the jars in the top cabinet she brought out the cognac that was kept for guests, though nobody had come to see them in ten years and the bottle stood nearly full, with dust on its shoulders. She poured a splash into the tea, hesitated, and added another.

Then she went into the dark hall, to her coat, took the pale glove from the pocket, and back in the kitchen set it on the table beside the cake, palm up, its fingers slightly curled. It fit when she pulled it on, and the thin leather quickly warmed. Under the lamp her left hand turned this way and that, made a fist, opened again: narrow, a stranger's, like that of a woman who goes to the sea and grabs her bag when she laughs. Natalie laughed without a sound, so it wouldn't carry over the television, and grabbed the edge of the table with the glove.

Then she took it off, smoothed out each finger, and laid it on her knee.

The plastic dome came off the cake with a click. She didn't bother with a knife: she got a tablespoon from the drawer, drove it into the side, right down by the base, broke off a piece and ate it. The frosting was greasy and so sweet it was almost bitter. She washed it down from the mug, and the cold tea and cognac burned her throat.

She went on slowly, from the edge toward the middle, working around the rose, which she was saving for last. The spoon left half-moon marks on the paper doily. Through the wall the audience clapped; now and then Mother coughed.

When half the cake was gone, the phone buzzed on the table and its screen lit up under the lamp.

*Happy birthday, Natalie! 36 is the perfect time for new possibilities. You've been pre-approved for a personal loan at a special rate. Apply in the app in just 2 minutes!*

Natalie read the message twice, then scooped up the rose with her spoon and ate it whole.
