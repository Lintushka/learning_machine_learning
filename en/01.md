# Chapter 1

Two women were sitting by the window and talking so loudly that Natalie knew how much a room with a sea view cost. The one facing the room said the number and laughed, as if the number were indecent.

Natalie sat in the corner, by the kitchen door. Every time the waiter shouldered the door open, warm air rolled over her, smelling of fried onions and a wet rag. The lamp above her table didn't work. Maybe no one had changed the bulb since summer. Or maybe no one here counted this table as a table. Natalie had picked it herself, from the doorway. She hadn't taken off her coat. The coat was gray, the sweater under it was gray too, and by evening her hair had matted into a dark knot at the back of her head. She kept touching it and couldn't find where it began. If anyone had looked into this corner, they would have seen the pizza first, and then, maybe, her hands.

No one looked.

The pizza was getting cold. Natalie tore off pieces with her fingers and chewed them slowly and angrily, without bothering to hide it. The cheese stretched from her lips to the plate, and she bit it through. With the pizza they had brought her a glass of red. Natalie couldn't remember the last time she had ordered wine, and she wasn't sure she had ever ordered it for herself. The wine was sour. She drank half of it right away and saved the rest.

The women were talking about men. One was cheap, but he was fun in bed. They said it just like that, "in bed," and one of them covered her mouth with her hand and the other didn't bother. The other man brought flowers every week, and that was annoying, because you had to put flowers somewhere and change their water, and after a week they stank. Then they talked about vacation. Somewhere hot, where you could lie around all day and do nothing. Someone had been given a bracelet for an anniversary, and it was the wrong bracelet.

The wrong bracelet. Natalie picked an olive off the pizza with her fingernail and crushed it against the roof of her mouth with her tongue.

She stared at them without the slightest embarrassment. It's easy from the dark, the way you look into other people's windows at night from an unlit room. The one facing the room had lips so full of filler that the upper one rested on the lower like the brim of a cap. Her lipstick was a loud, tacky scarlet, the color of a fire truck. Her forehead was smooth and motionless, as if it had been sealed in plastic. Her cheeks shone. Someone had drawn her eyebrows on thick and even, the way you draw with a marker. She laughed with only her mouth. The rest of her face took no part in it.

The other one sat half-turned toward Natalie. Her face was softer, her voice lower, and she kept touching her bag. The bag sat on the chair beside her: pale smooth leather, a gold clasp that gleamed even here. Whenever she laughed, her hand found the bag on its own and pressed it down against the seat, as if the bag might walk off. Natalie noticed it on the third or fourth laugh, and after that she waited for it. A laugh, a grab. A laugh, a grab. Natalie wanted the bag to fall. She wanted it to fall and spring open and spill everything onto the dirty floor, and she wanted the woman to crawl under the table on her knees and pick it all up.

They were her age or a little older. Natalie could tell by their necks and hands. Nobody injected their neck and hands. Close to forty. So people still had all of it at close to forty, the sea, the bracelets, the men in bed. They had probably had it before, too, and no one had ever said to them: what do you need that for.

Natalie thought they must be stupid. It was pleasant to think so. She also thought that the husband of the one with the lips was surely sleeping with someone younger, and that she knew it, and that was why she laughed so loudly. That was even more pleasant. Then she thought that the other one had been fat in school and the other kids had teased her, and now she had lost the weight and turned mean and was taking revenge on everyone with her bag. That one wasn't true, and Natalie knew it. But the thought was warm, like the wine, and she didn't want to let it go.

The waiter walked past her table for the third time. She raised her hand. He didn't see it, or he saw it and didn't stop. He was carrying two desserts to the window, and he leaned down and said something. Both women laughed up at him at once, and the one with the bag took hold of the bag.

The desserts were identical: a disk of chocolate with a scrap of gold leaf on top. Natalie knew the leaf was edible and tasted like nothing, and still she watched the woman lift it with her spoon and lay it on her tongue.

She looked down at her own plate. Natalie always left the crusts. Mother used to say only beggars ate the crusts, and then she used to say throwing bread away was a sin, and Natalie never found out which of these was true. So she left the crusts on her plate, and later she ate them when no one was looking. No one was looking now. She ate one and left the rest.

At the window they had moved on to children. The one with the lips had a daughter. The daughter didn't want to study what she was supposed to. She wanted to draw.

"So what did you do?" the other one asked.

"What could I do? Let her draw. It's her life."

Natalie stopped chewing.

The woman licked her spoon and said that when she was sixteen she had wanted to be a singer, can you imagine, a singer. And she laughed. The other one laughed too and reached for her bag.

The bag rocked. Something slid off it and fell under the table, softly, without a sound. A glove. Pale, the color of milky coffee, to match the bag. The gloves must have been lying on top, tucked under the strap, and one hadn't held on.

The woman didn't notice. She was laughing.

Natalie could see where the glove lay: by the leg of the chair, its fingers pointing at the window. She could have said, "Excuse me, you dropped this." Three words, not counting "excuse me." Natalie said them to herself, very politely and clearly, and out loud she said nothing. She finished her wine.

The women asked for the check, and it came right away. They argued for a long time over who would pay, and the one with the lips won. She held her phone to the card reader and didn't even glance at the total. Then they put on their things: coats, scarves, a long search for something in the bag, the car keys, another search. The other woman picked up a single glove from the chair, turned it over in her hand, tilted her head and looked at the floor.

Natalie took a crust from her plate and began to crumble it onto the tablecloth.

The woman didn't bend down. She shrugged and stuffed the glove into her pocket.

"Lose it?" the first one asked.

"Oh, forget it. It's in the car somewhere."

They left. The bell over the door rang, cold air ran along the floor, and the room went so quiet that Natalie could hear water running in the kitchen.

She sat a while longer. Then she got up as if she were going to the restroom, walked past the window, bent down as if to fix her boot, and picked up the glove. The leather was thin, almost like paper, and warm on top from the radiator. Inside, a slippery lining. The glove smelled of hand cream and of something sweet, the way it smells in the stores Natalie never went into. She slipped the glove into her coat pocket and kept walking, to the restroom, because she had told herself she was going to the restroom.

In the restroom she washed her hands and looked in the mirror. The light above it hummed. A woman in gray looked back at her, with a gray face and a smear of sauce on her chin. Natalie wiped off the smear. She wondered whether to give the glove to the waiter. He would drop it into a box of lost things, where somebody's umbrellas were already lying, and one child's mitten, and no one would ever come for it. Oh, forget it. So what difference did it make.

She went back to her table. The pocket sagged, though the glove weighed nothing.

Natalie looked at her phone. Six forty-one. She had come in at ten past six. She looked at the crusts, at the glass with its red ring at the bottom, at the black window where the room was reflected. Her table didn't show in the reflection. Only a piece of wall and the kitchen door.

It was time to go.

She had to ask for the check at the counter. The waiter looked at her as if she had just walked in off the street.

"Which table were you?"

"In the corner."

He searched the room for the corner and didn't find it right away.

Natalie paid with her card. The reader thought for a long time. She had time to imagine that there was no money on the card, and that the waiter was about to say so, out loud, to the whole empty room. The reader beeped. Approved. The waiter tore off the receipt, set it in front of her without looking, and went over to the window to put the chairs up on the tables.

***

Outside it was drizzling. Fine water hung in the air and settled on her face, and there was no point wiping it off. It was the end of October, when night comes before seven and the streetlights seem to shine only for themselves. Wet leaves were plastered to the pavement, and her soles slid on them.

She didn't put on her own gloves, which were gray and knitted and pilled. They stayed in her pocket with the keys, the list, and the other one.

She took the list out under a streetlight, though she knew it by heart. Mother wrote on the backs of old bills, in big letters, pressing hard, so that at every period her pen went through the paper.

*Milk (NOT skim)*

*Bread*

*Eggs. Check the dates!!*

*Butter*

*Green apples. GREEN*

*Dish soap*

*Batteries for the remote (not the ones from last time)*

And below, under a line: *Come straight home.*

The supermarket was across the street. The doors slid apart, and warm air and music no one was listening to washed over her. It was too bright inside. After the restaurant the light stung her eyes, and Natalie squinted at the price tags like an old woman.

She took a basket and went down the list. The milk took a long time. She had to find one that wasn't skim, with the latest date, and the fresh cartons were always at the back, so she had to reach into the cold. Next to her a young couple was choosing cheese. He held her from behind, one finger hooked through the belt loop of her jeans, and she laughed and said, "No, that one stinks." Natalie thought that a year from now he would be hooking his finger through someone else's belt loop. She thought it with pleasure, carefully, the way she had chosen the milk.

Bread. Eggs. She opened the carton and checked each egg, turning it in its cup with one finger. Once there had been a cracked one, and Mother had brought it up for a week. Butter. The apples were all yellowish green. Natalie stood over them for a minute, picking out the greenest, and knew anyway what she would be told.

By the shelves of cleaning products a little boy was lying on the floor, screaming. A woman stood over him with her phone and didn't look down. Natalie walked around them and thought that this woman knew nothing about children, and that her own child, Natalie's, would never lie on the floor like that. Then she thought that she would never have a child, and she took the cheapest dish soap off the shelf. She held it for a moment and put it back. She took a more expensive one. Mother called the cheap kind water.

The batteries hung by the registers. Natalie checked them against the slip of paper and couldn't remember which ones had been last time. She took some at random. They were almost certainly the wrong ones.

The list was done. She could go to the register.

Natalie didn't go to the register.

The cake fridge stood at the far end of the store, next to the frozen fish, humming. The cakes sat behind the glass on three shelves, each on a cardboard base with a paper doily, under a clear plastic dome. Chocolate, sponge, whipped cream, berries so glossy they looked varnished. On one of them someone had piped *With Love* in icing.

Natalie stood in front of the glass for a long time. Her reflection stood in it too, holding a basket with milk and eggs. A security guard walked by and looked at her, and so she opened the door, so that he wouldn't think she was just standing there.

She took a small cake. Not the cheapest. White, covered in buttercream, with a single rose in the middle. The rose was made of frosting, pink, with one edge slightly melted. There was no writing on it.

The girl at the register, who had a ring through her eyebrow, scanned the milk, the bread, the eggs, the butter, the apples, the soap, the batteries. Then the cake. Then she raised her eyes to Natalie. Natalie waited for her to say something.

"Do you need a bag?" the girl said.

"Yes."

Natalie set the cake on top, on the apples, so it wouldn't get crushed. At the exit the bag hit the turnstile anyway. She stopped and looked inside. The rose was fine.

It was twelve minutes to the building. The handles of the bag cut into her fingers right away, and Natalie kept switching it from hand to hand. She warmed the free hand in her pocket, and there it kept bumping into the glove, smooth, someone else's. She pulled her fingers back. A minute later they found it again.

At the crosswalk she stepped in a puddle. She hadn't seen it. It lay in the shadow of a billboard, flat and black, no different from the asphalt. Natalie stepped into it with her whole foot. Water poured into her boot at once, ice cold, and spread under her toes. Natalie stood in the middle of the puddle until the light began to blink, and then she walked on. Her boot squelched at every step.

Her sock was soaked through, and her tights under it. First her foot froze, then she couldn't feel it at all. Natalie walked, the bag knocking against her knee, and she thought that the eggs were probably broken by now, and about how she would dry out the boot, and that right now, this very minute, the woman with the bag was sitting in a warm car, feeling around under the seat for the other glove, not finding it, and not caring.

At the door of the building Natalie set the bag down on the wet step and reached for her keys. The keys were at the very bottom, under everything else. She pulled at them, and everything came out of the pocket with them at once: the list, both of her knitted gloves, and the pale one. The keys clinked against the step and slid into the gap of the boot grate. The gloves fell into the water.

Natalie crouched over them. She picked up the pale one first. She shook it off, wiped it on her coat, blew on it for no reason, and put it back in her pocket. Then she picked up her own, wet and heavy as rags. Then she spent a long time fishing the keys out of the grate with two fingers, and scraped a knuckle on the metal. The list was soaked. The bottom line had run, and *Come straight home* had turned into a blue smear.

She straightened up and for a second leaned her forehead against the door. The door was cold and smelled of paint. Somewhere upstairs a television was on.

It was theirs.

The apartment smelled of medicine and of something fried. The television was talking to the whole apartment: someone had cheated on someone, someone was crying, the audience was clapping.

"Finally," Mother said from the other room.

Natalie pulled off the wet boot. Her wet sock left a dark print on the floor.

Mother came out into the hall in her bathrobe, holding on to the wall, though there was no need to hold on to the wall.

"I've been alone all evening," she said. "My blood pressure's two hundred, everything's going black. I thought, that's it, I'm going to die and nobody will even know, and you're off somewhere. Do you understand that? There's no one but you to bring me so much as a glass of water."

"I understand."

"You don't understand anything." Mother stroked her wet sleeve. "Are you cold? My poor girl. Why do they keep you so late? People have no conscience at all."

"I got held up," Natalie said.

Mother unpacked the bag herself. She always unpacked it herself. She held the milk up to the light and read the label, moving her lips. She opened the egg carton and counted. Over the apples she said, "Well, I see," and nothing more, and that was worse than if she had said something. She set the batteries aside without looking at them.

Then she took out the cake.

She held it at arm's length by the cardboard base, the way you hold something that might drip on your robe.

"What's this?"

"A cake."

"I can see it's a cake. I'm asking what it's for."

Natalie said nothing. She stood in the kitchen doorway on one leg, because the other one, the wet one, was freezing on the bare floor.

"What do you need cake for?" Mother set it on the table and pushed it away from herself with one finger. "Have you seen your ass? Pretty soon you won't fit into a single skirt. You won't fit through the door. Who do you think I'm doing all this for, making you oatmeal with water, and you're out shopping for little cakes. Money doesn't grow on trees, you know."

"Mother," Natalie said. "It's just a cake."

Mother raised her eyebrows.

"Mother," she repeated. She didn't say it to Natalie. She said it off to the side, to the corner where the calendar with the kittens hung. "What a word. Like in prison. Not Mom, not Mommy. 'Mother.' I raised her, I never slept, I gave her everything I had. And what did I ever do to deserve this from my Natalie?"

The kittens didn't answer. Neither did Natalie.

Mother stood there a moment, sighed, and reached into the cabinet for her pills. She kept them in a plastic box with a compartment for each day. Every evening she sorted them out for the next day, and every evening she asked the same thing.

"What's the date today?"

"The twenty-ninth," Natalie said, and waited.

Mother shook two white pills and one yellow one into her palm.

"The twenty-ninth," she repeated, and snapped the little lid shut. "So tomorrow's the thirtieth. Wake me at seven, I have to get my blood drawn. And put that cake away, I don't want to see it. And turn off the light in the hall. This isn't a palace."

She went into her room. The television got louder.

Natalie took off the other boot, then her socks and tights, and stood barefoot on the cold floor. She didn't feel like changing, or like taking a shower. She turned off the light in the hall.

The teapot had been sitting on the stove since morning. The tea in it had steeped until it was black and had gone cold long ago. Natalie poured a full mug and tasted it. Bitter as bark. She opened the top cabinet and took a bottle of cognac from behind the jars. The cognac was kept for guests. There hadn't been any guests in ten years, and the bottle was nearly full, with dust on its shoulders. Natalie poured a splash into the mug. She thought about it and poured another.

Then she went out into the dark hall, to her coat, took the pale glove from the pocket, and came back to the kitchen. She sat down. She laid the glove on the table next to the cake. It lay palm up, its fingers slightly curled.

Natalie put it on her left hand. It fit. The leather was cold from outside and warmed at once. Natalie turned her hand under the lamp, made a fist, opened it. The hand in the glove was someone else's, narrow. The hand of a woman who went to the sea and grabbed her bag when she laughed. Natalie laughed without a sound, so it wouldn't carry over the television, and grabbed the edge of the table with the glove.

Then she took it off, smoothed out each finger, and laid it on her knee.

She took the plastic dome off the cake. It clicked. Natalie didn't bother with a knife. She took a tablespoon from the drawer, pushed it into the side of the cake, right down by the base, broke off a piece and ate it. The frosting was greasy and so sweet it was almost bitter. She washed it down with tea. The tea and cognac were cold, but they burned her throat on the way down.

She ate slowly, from the edge toward the middle, going around the rose. She was saving the rose for last. The spoon left half-moon marks on the paper doily. Through the wall the audience clapped. Now and then Mother coughed.

When half the cake was gone, the phone buzzed on the table. The screen lit up under the lamp.

*Happy birthday, Natalie! 36 is the perfect time for new possibilities. You've been pre-approved for a personal loan at a special rate. Apply in the app in just 2 minutes!*

Natalie read the message twice. Then she scooped up the rose with her spoon and ate it whole.
